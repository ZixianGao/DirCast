Abstract

A live esports broadcast needs a director who chooses which camera goes on air and a caster who talks over that choice, every second and without knowing what comes next. Streaming video language models have learned to commentate, but only over a broadcast that a human director has already produced. We study live broadcast production, in which a model receives the raw camera of every player and must both direct and commentate from the past alone. We present DirCast, a single streaming video language model that does both. Every second it scans all cameras at low resolution, decides with a hold-or-switch director whether and where to cut, and reads the chosen camera at high resolution before it speaks. Its context keeps what went on air and what was said and lets the other views expire, which bounds the cost of a stream of any length. We build a dataset of 27 professional Dota 2 matches with a camera for every hero, the human director's choices and the casters' commentary. DirCast directs better than rule-based and zero-shot directors including GPT-5, and its commentary wins 29.2 percent of comparisons against GPT-4o under the Proact-VL protocol, against 19.4 percent for the strongest of eleven streaming commentators that watch the human broadcast.

The task

From ten raw cameras to one live broadcast.

Existing streaming commentators start from a broadcast a human has already directed. DirCast starts from the raw camera of every player. Each second it decides what the audience sees and what the caster says about it, using only the past.

Method

One stream for directing and commentary.

A director and a commentator are not two systems that happen to run at the same time. The director decides what the audience sees, the commentator talks about what the audience sees, and both depend on what has been shown and said so far. DirCast uses one causal video language model for both roles.

01

One stream

The match enters as one token stream that grows by a chunk per second, in the order a human crew works: scan all cameras, pick one, then look closely at the picture that went on air.

02

Hold or switch

A gate decides whether to leave the current shot and a pointer decides where to go. Holding is the default; switching requires the gate to open.

\(P(c_t{=}k) = (1-g_t)\,\mathbf{1}[k{=}c_{t-1}] + g_t\,\pi_t(k)\)
03

The program is the memory

Camera views leave the context after \(W\) seconds; the on-air views, events and words stay. The model remembers the broadcast it produced, at a constant cost per second.

04

Grounding the game

A state token per camera carries the hero's health, recent damage and nearby enemies, and every event is tied to the camera that shows it.

05

Learning from human broadcasts

Perturbed program rollouts teach recovery from the model's own wrong shots, and event-anchored targets teach that action is worth showing.

06

A human rhythm

Thresholds for cutting and speaking follow the model's own running rates toward those of human crews, which keeps the program lively without training signal.

\(\tau_t = \tau_0 + \gamma\,(\bar r_t - r^{*})\)

Dataset

Every camera the director could have chosen.

A broadcast shows one camera at a time, and the cameras the director did not choose are lost. Esports make them recoverable: a professional Dota 2 match is fully described by its replay, so it can be filmed again from every hero's point of view and aligned with the broadcast that actually aired.

professional matches, split 20 / 3 / 4 by match

01 · RENDER
Ten camerasEach replay is played back and filmed by ten synchronized spectator clients, one following each hero.
02 · ALIGN
Broadcast to replayThe HUD clock and hero names are read from the broadcast and matched to the replay, pauses included.
03 · LABEL
Director and castersEach live second gets the on-air camera, the hero state and events, and the casters' words with timestamps.
04 · CLEAN
Verify and polishCarried-over labels are verified by a vision–language model; commentary is polished following Proact-VL.
